# Off the Record: DevTalk Retro Spec

Nov 1, 2026 · @Erik Tetland

## Talk overview

A facilitated, phone-based, anonymous retro on 2026 for the Saskatoon tech community, ending with everything shredded.

**Title:** Off the Record: A Live Retro on 2026

**Description (final):**

> A live, anonymous retro on 2026, for the humans of Saskatoon tech. Bring your phone and your feelings about the year, whatever they are. We'll see what we share, then shred it all.

**Logistics:** Saskatoon DevTalk, Wednesday, November 25, 2026. Slot is about 25–30 minutes, with a break between speakers. Around 40 guests, all technical people from the local scene.

## Intent and context

The goal is emotional, not productive: a safe place to express how the year felt and see that others in the community felt the same.

- **About humans, not roles.** The retro is about people in this community, not about work output or tech stacks, and not against AI: the right tool in the right place still works.
- **Facilitator, not lecturer.** The facilitator facilitates. No content, questions or framing before submissions open, to avoid biasing input. During submissions, only light nudges from a preset list.
- **Release, not production.** No action items or tickets. The ending is the shred.
- **Depth through recognition, not long sharing.** In 30 minutes with 40 people, most won't share deeply, and that's fine. The depth comes from reading a card and thinking "that's exactly how I felt." Don't try to cover everything. The time after the evening's last talk carries the conversation in person (the break between talks is too short).
- **No presumption of a yearly event.** It could recur, but that isn't promised. The public repo lets anyone run it again without depending on one person or one host company.

## Session flow

The facilitator advances phases; every phone follows. The facilitator always has a back button (card or phase). The room has no way to ask to stay or go back from a phone: the room's reactions set the pace, and the facilitator watches them. In look-up phases, phones say "look up". Timings target 25 minutes (table below). The app runs each part on a talk clock; when a part is over, the facilitator is asked to confirm the move ("Ready for grouping?"), and the projector says "Waiting for the facilitator to confirm" so the room knows nothing is broken (except at "Ready to shred?": the finale stays wordless). A second tap on a move someone already made is ignored.

0. **Teaser, then demo.** Two phases come before Join. The session starts on the **teaser**: a short page (a title and up to four lines, from the demo file, with a plain "Starting soon." when it has none; a line can strike words through) that phones and the projector show to anyone who stumbles on the site before the event. Nothing from the demo reaches anyone while the session is on the teaser, and it is outside the talk plan, so leaving it never starts the talk clock. The facilitator moves on to the **demo** while people arrive. The demo is shown on the projector only; phones just see "Starting soon". It plays on a loop at a relaxed pace (about 70 seconds a round, the server moving the steps on so every projector shows the same one) until the facilitator takes over: Next or Back takes it off the loop, a switch puts it back (starting again from the first step, so the room sees it begin at once), and starting the talk clock while it is looping takes it off the loop and back to the first step. It walks through the whole app in five steps on prepared content (see Demo stage below): notes being written, picking some to share, the grouping happening, reacting, and the reveal. The facilitator can tap through the steps (next and back) and then moves on to Join; the demo's shared notes join the session as ordinary cards.
1. **Join.** QR code on the projector. Minimal logistics only: it's anonymous, nothing is kept on our server, and you can always speak aloud instead.
2. **Open submissions.** One short prompt, no categories, phrased as something to do: "Write down something you experienced, felt, or were surprised by in 2026." Nudges from a preset list appear as rotating placeholder text in the input; whenever submissions go quiet for about 10 seconds, the AI picks the nudge for the least-covered direction. If the mix is already varied, no nudge is shown. The demo's cards count toward that coverage like any other card. Participants first write as many private notes as they like, freely and without self-censoring; notes never leave the phone. Then they intentionally pick up to 3 to submit as cards (anything still in the writing box when they go to pick is saved as a note first, so nothing typed is lost), about 140 characters each (hard cap 200). A loose local check softly flags possible identifiers on the picked notes; the author decides. Once someone has shared all 3, the time warnings stop and their unshared notes stay listed, read-only, under what they shared, until they're wiped like any other private note. Projector shows "on your phone". Submissions get 5 minutes. The facilitator sees a live count; when the time is up they are asked "Ready for grouping?" and the window stays open until they confirm, and "+1 minute" extends it (and clears the prompt).
3. **Cluster.** The AI groups cards into themes, with near-duplicates in one group. The facilitator approves cluster names. The stream is ordered later, once reactions are in.
4. **React.** Each phone shows one card at a time, shuffled per person, so every distinct thought gets a fair chance. Near-duplicates in a group don't each need a full chance; their reactions are pooled. Tap one or more emojis, and optionally write a comment (up to 200 characters), then go to the next card. A comment alone is enough; a first send with neither an emoji nor a comment is refused. A comment is stored with no author, one per phone per card. "◀ Previous" goes back to change a card's reactions: the first send for a card returns a random receipt that the phone keeps, and sending again with it replaces those reactions and that card's comment (sending nothing with the receipt clears both; a comment the facilitator removed stays removed). A receipt covers one card's reactions and is never tied to the phone, so a person's reactions stay unlinkable across cards. Changing doesn't use up the one pass. Each person makes one pass, one card per group. Reactions get 5 minutes, with "+1 minute" as for submissions. The facilitator is asked "Ready for the walk-through?" when the time is up, or as soon as every connected phone has finished, and confirms. Reactions are still accepted for 10 seconds after the room moves on, so a send in flight isn't lost.
5. **Walk through the stream (look-up phase).** Phones say "look up". It opens on an overview slide before the first card: titled "Themes" (so it's clear the AI grouped the cards and found what they share), biggest first (any "first reactions?" question is asked aloud, not projected), so the room can take in the whole before going card by card. The facilitator sets the pace: the timers are hints they can override. The cards then advance by themselves on the projector, each showing its emoji mix and up to 5 of its comments (in the order they were sent, oldest first, with "+N more" for the newest; editing a comment keeps its place). A crowded card shrinks to fit the screen: its text, comments, haiku and spacing step down together, never below a readable floor, and if it still doesn't fit, it shows fewer comments (the oldest), with the rest counted in "+N more". The facilitator has Pause, Back and Next. The walk-through has a fixed finish, minute 21.5 on the talk clock (never shorter than 3 minutes): each card gets the walk-through's time left, less 20 seconds kept for the last slide, divided by the cards left (the overview counts as one), never under 15 seconds. A pause doesn't move the finish; it eats into later cards, and resuming gives the card a fresh share. If time runs out with cards left, it skips to the last slide, "Anything else? Out loud." (20 seconds; spoken, with no anonymous route), and then asks the facilitator "Ready for the finale?". After the walk-through's time is up, Back or Next onto a card (not the last slide) holds that card until the facilitator resumes or moves on; resuming it gives it a fresh share (at least 15 seconds), after which it goes to the last slide. Next onto the last slide runs its 20 seconds. Skipped cards' reactions still count in the finale. A thin bar on each card drains as its time runs, with no numbers: it is the one per-card signal the room sees, so someone speaking can see it coming and the facilitator can pause. The facilitator sees the comments on the current and next card, so they can remove one before it is projected. Aim for 4–6 clusters. The facilitator's view shows "N cards left · ~M:SS each" throughout, so skipping is never a surprise. The facilitator's view also suggests a couple of open questions per card for when the room is quiet (never projected). People speak aloud by raising a physical hand.
6. **Finale.** The room's whole emoji mix projected as one wordless visual, an emotional fingerprint of the year. A moment of quiet. With a full room (about 400 reactions) it takes about 12 seconds before the facilitator is asked "Ready to shred?": the replay about 6, the pooling 2.5, then 3 of quiet. The finale and the shred together take about 30 seconds, not counting that tap; both are paced slow, then faster in the middle, then slow again, so they stay calm. The facilitator can tap "Speed up ⏩" during either: the rest of that part plays three times faster (once; a second tap does nothing more), and the prompt or the closing screen comes sooner to match.
7. **Shred.** Everything is visibly destroyed and actually deleted. It opens with a plain "Delete everything?" dialog and a cursor clicking Delete, to say what is really happening on the server before the ritual shows it. Visual: a literal shredder; the pooled emojis, then the cards behind them, are pulled down through a slot and fall away as strips (narrow enough, about 1.2% of the screen width, that no whole word survives; they sway as they come out, flutter and crumple as they fall, and pile up in a mesh wastebasket), ending on a black screen and a short beat of silence, after which the closing screen fades in by itself. With a full room it takes about 18 seconds: the dialog about 2.5, the sheets about 11 however many cards (the pooled emojis, then at most 6 sheets of cards; the first and last go through slowest), then about 4.5 for the last strips to settle (about 1 second), the fade (about 2) and the black (about 1.5). Fewer cards take less. Back can't undo the shred, so it needs a confirm.
8. **Closing (optional).** What the app does, what was left out, and "what do you think?" The projector shows a QR code to a pinned GitHub Discussions thread for feedback (github.com/ert485/off-the-record/discussions/1). Posting there needs a GitHub account and is not anonymous, which is said in the thread itself; an anonymous feedback box in the app would contradict "nothing is kept", so there isn't one.

Talk clock: the facilitator starts it with one tap ("Start talk clock") at the first slide; it never restarts. Its length is 25 minutes, or whatever `TALK_MINUTES` is set to when the server starts. If they forget, it starts by itself when the room first moves on from the demo, backdated to where the plan says that moment should be, so the pace marker is still meaningful.

The walk-through is the overrun risk: a lively one eats the closing and buffer first.

Pace indicator: an ahead/behind marker, measured on the talk clock, showing where the session is against where it should be to finish on time, with a short label ("on pace", "~2 min behind") and an ⓘ that explains it on tap. No phase names. Facilitator view only: on the projector, a visible "behind" would make people hold back.

Section timer: the projector shows the time left for the current part (submissions, reactions, walk-through), so the room knows how long a part is meant to take. It disappears when time runs out rather than showing "over", for the same reason as the pace indicator. Submissions and reactions count down to the time the facilitator was asked at (so "+1 minute" moves it); the walk-through's timer counts down to its fixed finish on the talk clock, so going back to it (from the finale, say) doesn't restart it. When a part is over and the facilitator hasn't confirmed, the timer is gone and the projector says "Waiting for the facilitator to confirm" (the timer also stays hidden whenever that line shows).

## Demo stage

A scripted demo runs on the projector before Join, so the room sees the whole flow before they use it. It is prepared content, not live: it never touches the phones (they show "Starting soon") and nothing the demo shows is stored except its shared notes.

- **Content** comes from a data file: `demo/local.json` if it exists and is valid, else the generic `demo/example.json`. The facilitator's own file is gitignored but deployed (the deploy copies untracked files). An invalid local file prints the fixed line `demo: local file invalid, using the example` and falls back; a missing or invalid example stops the server at startup. A file holds 1 to 5 notes (1 to 200 characters each, 1 to 3 of them marked to share), a theme name (up to 60 characters) and haiku (up to 120), the reactions shown (three or so of the 16 presets plus one keyboard emoji), and a reveal (an emoji mix of emoji and counts). The demo shows no comments: an older file's `react.comment` and `reveal.comments` are ignored, not an error.
- **Teaser first.** The session starts on the teaser (the data file's optional `teaser`: a title of up to 60 characters and up to 4 lines of up to 160, markup included; without one, "Off the Record" and "Starting soon."). In a line, `~~words~~` show struck through: a thin, muted red line, the words still readable (an unpaired `~~` stays as typed). The projector and the phones both show the strike. The projector shows the title large with the lines below, centred, with no join QR, waiting line or section timer. Nothing from the demo is sent while the session is on the teaser.
- **Five steps**: notes, pick, group, react, reveal. Leaving the teaser starts them on a loop: the server moves each step on after 13, 16, 16, 12 and 12 seconds (about 70 seconds a round), wrapping from the reveal back to the notes, so every projector shows the same step. Each step plays its entrance as it arrives (including the wrap) and settles at least 3 seconds before the next. The facilitator can tap through instead (`demoNext`, `demoBack`; refused outside the demo), which takes it off the loop; a switch (`demoAuto`) puts it back, starting again from the notes with their entrance (a second tap while it already loops changes nothing), and starting the talk clock while it is looping takes it off the loop and back to the notes (a demo already stepped by hand is left where it is). Back, or a reload partway through a step, shows the step settled. There is no step counter on screen. The projector view carries the whole script; the facilitator's view carries only the step number and name; phones get nothing.
- **The notes step shows the writing.** It opens on the prompt and an empty writing box with its placeholder; after about 2.5 seconds the notes appear one at a time, about 0.9 seconds apart.
- **The pick step ends on the sending, not the card.** Each shared note is checked in turn (its check fills, about 0.8 seconds apart) while the Share button counts them ("Share 1 card", "Share 2 cards"), goes from disabled to enabled and is tapped, and the picked notes fold together into one plain sealed envelope (nothing written on it) that slides up out of the phone. The phone then shows "Shared." above the notes left behind, with no "you can still share" line. No card is shown in this step, and the envelope carries no text.
- **The group step shows the grouping happen.** The first shared note arrives alone in a plain folder with a blank tab. About 2.5 seconds later the tab's theme name appears a few characters at a time, then the haiku one segment at a time, about 0.9 seconds apart. Everything is settled at least 3 seconds before the step ends.
- **The react step has no comment box.** Presets are tapped one by one, then the keyboard emoji is added. The caption reads "React with emojis. At the end, add a thought if you like." The reveal shows the card with its emoji mix and haiku, and no comments.
- **What carries over.** Leaving the demo forward adds every shared note as an ordinary card (random id, no author, not counted against any phone), once each, in a random order, even if the facilitator goes back and forward again. The demo's reactions never enter the session, so they never show in the walk-through or the finale.

## App spec

Three views, one small server. Keep it minimal: about 40 users needs no heavy realtime infrastructure.

**What the app must get right**

These came out of building it and the practice runs. Check any change against them; the details further down are how each one is met today.

- **Every promise on screen is literally true.** Wording follows what the code actually does: "Notes you don't share never leave your phone", "Everything on our server is shredded tonight", notes "stay on this phone until you choose to share them". Where the app can't guarantee something (a phone that's offline at the shred), it tells the person what they can do ("To be sure, close this tab") rather than claiming more. A claim that can't be made true gets reworded, not kept.
- **Nothing a person does is lost or hidden from them.** Text typed but not saved still counts as a note. Shared cards stay visible as "Already shared". Reactions can be revisited and changed. No button leads to an empty step, and no action makes something silently disappear.
- **Every screen says what to do now and what happens next.** Prompts start with a verb. After an action, the phone says what's left ("You can still share 1 more") or that it's waiting for others. Controls that matter are visible where they're used, with accurate how-tos (the "＋ Add your own emoji" button and how to reach the emoji keyboard). Write for someone seeing it for the first time, with no explanation from the front.
- **Time is a visible hint, never pressure.** The room sees how long a part is meant to take, but never a per-card number, "over time" or "behind"; the walk-through's draining bar (no numbers) is the one per-card signal. "A visible hint" covers submissions and reactions; the walk-through is a timed reveal with a pause. The facilitator sees the pace and the cards left with each one's share, each explained on tap, and decides; timers are hints they override.
- **The facilitator view works at a glance for a first-time facilitator.** The same layout and colours in every phase; buttons say where they go ("Start reactions ▶", "◀ Back one card"); every number explains itself on tap (ⓘ) instead of headings filling the screen; status messages give the real reason (an empty round, not "no API key"); common actions take one tap ("Approve all"); nothing collapses or moves under a finger; nothing is shown that does nothing (no Back or Done after the shred).
- **Defaults keep the facilitator's influence, and pressure on the room, low.** Nudges start off. The last slide, "Anything else? Out loud.", invites speaking out loud. The walk-through opens on the themes as a whole before any single card. Prompts for a quiet room stay on the facilitator's screen.
- **The rituals feel real, and don't drag.** The shred has to read as actual destruction: a literal "Delete" moment, strips too narrow for any word to survive, physical motion into a bin. The finale's scale is continuous: the particles add up to the pools they become. Endings stay brief, and the closing fades in by itself.
- **Readable from the back of the room.** Anything the room has to read (the late-join QR caption, the last slide) is large and short. Longer explanations go in fine print or are said aloud.
- **Built to run again, by anyone.** After the shred, one confirmed step starts a new session. The code is public, and feedback goes to a public thread that says plainly it isn't anonymous.

**Design rules**

- No dot voting. Emoji reactions are the signal, and they capture mixed feelings.
- Emojis: 16 preset reactions (🙋 me too, 🫂 sending care, 😂 laughed, 🥲 bittersweet, 😢 sad, 😤 frustrated, 😰 anxious, 🎉 glad for you, 🔥 this is fine, 🤯 mind blown, 🫠 melting, 😮‍💨 relief, 💪 proud of you, ❤️ love this, 🙏 grateful, 👀 noticed that), in a 4-column grid plus any emoji from the phone keyboard (covers inside jokes).
- The reaction stack is shuffled per person so reactions spread evenly. Advancing is a plain "next", never a left/right swipe that feels like accept or reject.
- Too few cards (under about 10–15): stream every card and spend longer on each, still clustering near-duplicates. Too many: a curated stream with "+N similar".
- Phone vibrates on each phase change where the browser allows it (Android, after a first tap: the join screen has an "I'm here" button for that). iPhones don't support web vibration, so every phone also flashes its screen.
- Phones carry no steering signals (no stay, go back or move on): the room's reactions set the pace, and projecting a request while someone talks would be harsh. No "this one hit me": the emoji mix already shows how much a card landed.
- No digital raise hand; physical hands keep the room human.
- No open anonymous Q&A during the walk-through. Comments are written only while reacting, about the card being reacted to, and are shown (up to 5 per card) when the walk-through reaches that card.

**Build notes**

- One server, SQLite, SSE or 2-second polling. As built: Node 22 with no framework, the built-in `node:sqlite`, plain HTML and JavaScript with no build step. SSE sends each screen a full snapshot of what it may see (phones never receive hidden cards, comments, unapproved theme names or the pace marker), so a phone that drops off LTE just catches up. Two dependencies: the Anthropic SDK and a QR code library.
- Hosting: an EC2 instance in Erik's personal AWS account at `otr.tetl.ca` (DNS at the tetl.ca registrar points to an Elastic IP). Caddy in front for HTTPS with all of its logging discarded; the app logs nothing about requests. No EBS snapshots, no AWS Backup plan, no VPC flow logs, no load balancer. The instance is terminated after the talk, which destroys its disk.
- Phones keep private notes, and the cards they've already shared (listed separately as "Already shared" so people can still see what they sent), in the tab's sessionStorage: they survive a reload on a flaky connection. They're wiped when the shred reaches the phone or the tab closes. A phone that is offline at the shred can't hear about it, so every session has a random id, and a phone wipes notes written in a different session as soon as it reconnects. Closing the tab is the sure way, and the phone says so: on the unshared notes ("To be sure, close this tab") and on the shred and closing screens ("You can close this tab").
- Phones warn twice before submissions close: at 2 minutes ("leave time to pick what you share") and at 1 minute ("pick what to share now"), so a slow writer still has time to pick.
- Any emoji from the keyboard is offered as a clear "＋ Add your own emoji" button under the presets, which opens a box and says how to reach the emoji keyboard (a practice run found the bare box easy to miss). Emojis of your own that you've used on earlier cards appear as one-tap chips on later cards (up to 6, most recent first, kept on the phone and wiped like the notes); adding a 7th replaces the oldest one not picked on that card. The box takes emojis only: letters are cleared with a hint to switch to the emoji keyboard, and a preset typed from the keyboard selects that preset.
- The projector (the facilitator's laptop only) loads Noto Color Emoji from Google Fonts; participants' phones load nothing from third parties.
- Projector animations move things only by transform and opacity, so the laptop's GPU composites them (the walk-through's draining bar scales rather than changing width). The finale's emoji particles and the shred's paper strips are drawn on a canvas rather than being hundreds of page elements: each shred sheet is drawn once and cut into strips, and landed strips are drawn into the bin once. Both play on the server's clock for their part, so a projector that reloads partway through picks up where the room is.
- Test on LTE; don't rely on venue wifi. Render emojis on the projector in one font (such as Noto Color Emoji) so phone styles don't clash.
- QR code must be readable from the back row; projector text legible at a distance.
- Rehearse twice. Tech run, solo, by Nov 18 (code near-final): laptop as facilitator plus 3–4 devices, one on LTE; check phase sync, back, clustering, real deletion, projector legibility. Full run, Nov 20–22, 5–10 people on their own phones on the real 25-minute clock; check timings, nudges, false flags, controls, finale and shred feel. Ask testers: "Did anything make you hold back?"

**Facilitator access (no logins)**

- "One facilitator" means one secret, not one device: a facilitator token, set as an env var for this one event.
- Facilitator view at a secret URL like `/facilitate?t=…`, bookmarked on both laptop and phone. Both work at once, so a crash never locks the facilitator out. Without the right token the URL returns "not found", and pages send no referrer, so the token can't leak through a link.
- Participants only ever get the join code. The facilitator view has no submit option; the facilitator can submit their own card from a participant tab like anyone else.

## Facilitation logic

Anonymizing happens locally on each phone, with no AI. The AI only clusters and orders cards after they're submitted.

**Pre-submission check (local, no AI)**

- Loose heuristics on the phone (case-insensitive matching against a list of local company names and a few thousand common first names, minus names that are also common words like "will" or "mark", and minus AI assistants' names like "Claude", which a practice run flagged) show a soft indicator: "You might have named or identified someone here. Consider rephrasing if so."
- The author decides whether to edit. Nothing is blocked, and the text stays on the phone until submitted.
- The same check applies to comments.
- No cloud AI anonymizing and no facilitator moderation queue. Accepted tradeoff: a card that slips past the heuristics may be projected. The only safety net is that the facilitator can hide any card before it's projected. Hidden cards leave the stream and reaction stack and are shredded with everything else.

**Clustering and the stream**

- Groups submitted cards into themes, including near-duplicates when there are few cards.
- Themes: about one per 12 cards, never fewer than 2 or more than 5. Grouping is by the shared experience or feeling underneath, not surface topic; no catch-all theme; themes roughly balanced. The tested prompt and schema are `groupingPrompt` and `SCHEMA` in `server/cluster.js` (from `spike/haiku-themes.mjs`); only the theme-count sentence varies with the card count. Model Claude Haiku 5.5; the full grouping runs at effort `high`, streamed (`max_tokens` 64000, 120 s timeout, one retry).
- A haiku per theme, written in the same call: three short lines, about 5-7-5, joined by " / ", capturing the shared feeling rather than any one card's details. Its one everyday image comes from the world the theme's cards describe (for a theme of one or two cards, that card's world, without quoting it), never a season, weather, temperature, or place stereotype the cards don't mention. Capped at 120 characters; a longer one is dropped (a cut haiku reads broken). The facilitator can edit or clear it beside the name, and approving a theme approves its name and haiku together. Projector only, never the phones: it shows small below the emoji mix and comments on that theme's cards, never on the overview or the last slide, and only once the theme is approved. It is split at " / ", with each slash shown, dimmed, and lines break only between segments: one line when it fits, otherwise breaking at a slash. A segment too wide for its line shrinks (down to 70%) rather than wrap, and wraps only if it still doesn't fit. The local fallback has no haiku.
- Neutral cluster labels, approved by the facilitator before projection.
- Grouping starts before submissions close: at the 1-minute warning, the server runs the full grouping (effort `high`, about 40 seconds for 60 cards) on the cards so far, once per session; "+1 minute" afterwards doesn't rerun it. The facilitator can read and approve theme names and haiku while people finish submitting. If the room confirms before it finishes, grouping waits for it; if it never ran (confirmed before the warning), it runs on entering grouping.
- Late arrivals are placed and assessed in one quick call. On confirming "Ready for grouping?", the cards that have no group yet go to Claude Haiku 5.5 with the existing themes and groups (visible cards only) in one call at effort `low` (`max_tokens` 4000, not streamed, 20 s timeout, no retry; aim under 10 seconds). The tested prompt and schema are `LATE_SYSTEM` and `LATE_SCHEMA` in `server/cluster.js` (from `spike/late-arrivals.mjs`, unrevised). For each late card it returns where it goes (an existing group as a near-duplicate, or an existing theme as a new group) and whether it might change the picture: it fits no theme well, or several late cards together suggest a theme that isn't there. With no API key, or after a failed or skipped grouping (one card per group), there is no quick call: every late card becomes a new group in that theme at once, with no flags; Retry is the way back to the AI.
  - Nothing flagged: the late cards are placed at once; names, haiku and approvals stay.
  - Something flagged: the facilitator sees just the flagged late cards, each with the model's one-line reason (it can paraphrase a card's text; it is shown only to the facilitator and shredded with everything else), and chooses **keep the themes and add the late arrivals** (every late card placed as suggested; one without a valid placement goes to an unapproved "Other" theme as its own group) or **regroup everything** (a full high-effort run of about 40 seconds; names and haiku are approved again). The room can't move on until one is chosen, and the projector shows "Waiting for the facilitator to confirm" meanwhile.
  - If the quick call fails or is skipped, every late card is flagged with the reason ("The quick check didn't finish: …"), and the same choice applies.
  - How many cards came late doesn't decide it; whether they'd change the themes does.
- Why not always regroup: most cards arrive in the last minute or two (the warnings tell people to pick then), and a full run would reshuffle themes the facilitator has already read, and take about 40 seconds while the room waits. The quick call keeps the early work unless the facilitator decides the picture really changed.
- Orders cards into one stream: split clusters first, a few representative cards per cluster, the rest as "+N similar". Keep the ordering rule simple and transparent, since order is a form of editorial framing. The rule as built (`server/stream.js`): themes in order of total reactions; inside a theme, distinct thoughts in order of reactions; with 15 or fewer distinct thoughts, show them all, otherwise the top 3 per theme, the last one carrying "+N more like this". Near-duplicates always show as one card with "+N similar".
- Both calls send each card as a short number (1 to N, in id order, so the numbers say nothing about who wrote what), never its real id, and map the answer back: with the random 31-bit card ids, Claude Haiku renumbered the cards 1 to N in most test runs, which put every card in "Other".
- Hidden cards are never sent to the AI. If clustering fails (or there's no API key), every card becomes its own group in one theme and the facilitator names it; a Retry button re-runs it. While the AI is grouping, the facilitator can "Skip the AI" and get that same fallback at once, so a slow or stuck API never holds up the room. Skipping a quick call that is already running counts as a failure: the late cards are flagged, and keeping puts them in "Other". (After a failed, skipped or local grouping there is no quick call to skip: late cards join the fallback theme without any call.)
- Going back to grouping never regroups: cards that came in with no group yet are placed by the same quick call, and existing groups, reactions and approved names stay. Hiding a card doesn't regroup either, so reactions stay attached to their cards.
- Budget about 10 seconds per card shown.

**Nudges (preset, any year)**

Shown as rotating placeholder text. As built (`server/nudges.js`): with fewer than 5 cards, phones rotate all six. After that, once submissions are quiet for 10 seconds, Claude Haiku tags each visible card with the directions it touches, and phones show only the nudge for the first direction no card covers; if every direction is covered, no nudge. Nudges start off in every session; the facilitator turns them on with the toggle, and their view says which nudge phones are showing. The AI matches cards to labels and shows the nudge for the least-covered one; "good" and "hard" are a balancing pair. No "someone who…" nudges, since they invite names. Refine after practice rounds.

| Label | Nudge text |
| --- | --- |
| outside work | Something away from the keyboard. |
| small moment | A small moment that stuck with you. |
| change | Something that changed for you. |
| connection | A time you felt connected, or didn't. |
| good | Something you're glad about. |
| hard | Something that was hard. |

**Finale (replaces the written summary)**

A written AI summary would turn the ending into production and have the AI speaking for the room. Instead, project the room's whole emoji mix as one wordless visual.

- An emotional fingerprint of the year: "this is all of us" without a deliverable. Visual: every reaction arrives as an emoji in the order reactions happened (about a 6-second replay, a few at first, quicker in the middle, a few again at the end), sized so that each type's particles together cover about the same area as the pool they become (so a handful of reactions fill the screen with big emojis, and many fill it with small ones), then pools into one emoji per type, sized by count (area, not radius). Rare keyboard emojis stay small. No count labels: size alone carries scale, keeping the finale wordless and number-free. Optional: a faint trail of the original particles around each pool (not built; cut for time).
- Let it sit for a moment, then shred it with everything else.
- No note-taker. Recording spoken points would undermine "off the record" and make people self-censor.

## Privacy and the shred

The shred is the core of the vibe: it makes anonymity believable and turns the retro into release rather than production.

Why it works: impermanence invites candor in a small community where everyone knows everyone; ending in destruction says the point was saying it and being heard; it's a ritual with a clear emotional close; and it leaves nothing to maintain.

**Requirements**

- No login, no names stored. Unsubmitted notes never leave the phone and are discarded: wiped when the shred reaches the phone or the tab closes (see Build notes for a phone that's offline at the shred).
- Cards are stored with no author and no timestamp, under random ids and in id order (never the order they arrived), so their order doesn't reveal which cards one phone submitted together. The server knows each phone only by a random id, and keeps counts against it (cards submitted, cards reacted to), never which cards or reactions.
- The shred deletes the SQLite database and everything else in the data directory (the app writes nothing anywhere else), then keeps the pooled emojis and card text in memory only for the projector's animation (about 18 seconds). `test/shred.test.js` proves it: it runs a whole session and then checks that no file, log line or screen still holds any of it, and that the server printed no IP address.
- Identifier checks run locally; the author decides exactly what gets shared.
- After submission, cards go to Anthropic's API (Claude Haiku 5.5, chosen for speed) for clustering. Softened promise, stated in one sentence: the provider doesn't train on the cards and deletes them within its retention window (about 30 days); everything on our server is gone tonight. Checked Nov 19 against Anthropic's privacy center: API inputs and outputs are deleted within 30 days, except content its safety systems flag for a usage-policy violation, which can be kept up to 2 years. Haiku 5.5 is not one of the "Covered Models" with special retention rules.
- The shred must be literally true on our side: delete all server data, including backups and any files on disk. Say so.
- Turn off server access logs (no IP addresses kept) and skip hosting analytics.
- Be honest about the limit: in a room of about 40 people, a card's content can identify someone even with names removed.
- No published results.

**Trust disclosure (keep it short)**

One slide or sentence in plain language, plus the repo link and QR code for anyone who wants to check. On the projector, a short version is big ("Anonymous: no logins, no names. Notes you don't share never leave your phone. Everything on our server is shredded tonight.", each line literally true) and the full disclosure below it is fine print, read aloud. No lengthy security defence; it would read as suspicious and eat the minimal opening. Optionally, one respected community member reviews the repo beforehand and the facilitator mentions it in one line. The disclosure names every power the facilitator has, including seeing cards and comments before they're shown and removing any of them.

Draft (one slide, read aloud during Join, about 20 seconds):

> Anonymous: no logins, no names, no IP logs. Your notes stay on your phone unless you pick them to submit. Submitted cards go to Anthropic (Claude Haiku) for grouping; they don't train on them and delete them within 30 days. I see cards and comments before they're shown and can remove any of them to prevent abuse. Everything on our server is shredded tonight. Code: \[short repo URL\]

**Facilitator cues (said aloud, not on screen)**

From the practice run, things the facilitator says rather than the app showing. Say less from the front: the app does most of the work.

- **Intro: spoken over the teaser and demo, no slides, under a minute.**
  - This isn't a typical dev talk: it's an experiment, and the app does most of the work. Stop me any time if how it works is unclear.
  - Why: 2026 was a weird year. Harvest the weirdness (good, bad and strange) and turn it into some community bonding.
  - Make it a safe place for genuine thoughts.
  - Point at the struck "shred" on the teaser. It doesn't mean tearing anything apart; it means letting it go, released into the wind. Nothing feeds a backlog or an action item due in January.
  - Don't steer the tone toward negative feelings. Good, weird and heavy are all welcome: framing before submissions biases what people write.
  - Feedback on how to improve this is for the end (there's a QR code then, and the facilitator stays after the next talk).
- **Say up front that it may go quiet** while people write and read, and that this is expected.
- **Don't ask "who's done a retro?"** It makes people expect the retro their own team runs.
- **Don't read or explain the slides.** When the room laughs or gasps, acknowledge it in a word and move on. Speak mainly to keep things moving, or to wrap up a conversation that's running long.
- **Don't warn people off inappropriate emojis.** It invites them. If one appears, skip past it.
- **About people, not against AI.** 2026's weirdness for this crowd is partly AI, and some cards will say so. Don't pile on, and don't frame the talk as against AI: the app itself uses AI where it fits (the grouping and the haiku). If it comes up, one line is enough: "The right tool in the right place still works; tonight was about the people."
- **At the closing** (replacing the old "that was me too" cue):
  - Start with a deep, unhurried thank you, for trusting the room with what people wrote.
  - Then the facilitator's own closing thought, in a few sentences. This run's: make good friends along the way. The facilitator has worked with and learned from many good people who stayed in the city and built amazing things, and a city this size makes it impossible not to keep running into them. Everyone in the room has people like that around them too; they just have to find them and connect.
  - Then: "If a card stuck with you, find someone after the next talk and talk about it." When speaking, name the next speaker.
  - Not "at the break": the break between talks is too short for a real conversation. The time for it is after the following talk.
  - The earlier line about running this with coworkers and family is dropped. The friends thought says the same thing better.
