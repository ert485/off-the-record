# Off the Record: DevTalk Retro Spec

Nov 1, 2026 · @Erik Tetland

## Talk overview

A facilitated, phone-based, anonymous retro on 2026 for the Saskatoon tech community, ending with everything shredded.

**Title:** Off the Record: A Live Retro on 2026

**Description (final):**

> A live, anonymous retro on 2026, for the humans of Saskatoon tech. Bring your phone and your feelings about the year, whatever they are. We'll see what we share, then shred it all.

**Logistics:** Saskatoon DevTalk, Wednesday, November 25, 2026. Slot is about 25–30 minutes, with a break between speakers. Around 40 guests, all technical people from the local scene.

## Intent and context

The goal is emotional, not productive: a safe place to express how the year felt and see that others in the community felt the same.

- **About humans, not roles.** The retro is about people in this community, not about work output or tech stacks.
- **Facilitator, not lecturer.** Erik facilitates. No content, questions or framing before submissions open, to avoid biasing input. During submissions, only light nudges from a preset list.
- **Release, not production.** No action items or tickets. The ending is the shred.
- **Depth through recognition, not long sharing.** In 30 minutes with 40 people, most won't share deeply, and that's fine. The depth comes from reading a card and thinking "that's exactly how I felt." Don't try to cover everything. The break afterward carries the conversation in person.
- **No presumption of a yearly event.** It could recur, but that isn't promised. The public repo lets anyone run it again without depending on one person or one host company.

## Session flow

The facilitator advances phases; every phone follows. Erik always has a back button (card or phase). Participants can suggest going back, and the projector shows the live count, so the room can steer while Erik decides. In look-up phases, phones mainly say "look up" but keep a few light taps; it's fine that using a phone is visible. Timings target 25 minutes (table below). The app runs each part on a talk clock; when a part is over, Erik is asked to confirm the move ("Ready for grouping?"), and the projector says "Waiting for the facilitator to confirm" so the room knows nothing is broken. A second tap on a move someone already made is ignored.

0. **Demo.** The first phase, before Join, shown on the projector; phones just see "Starting soon". It is a placeholder in this step and gets its content later.
1. **Join.** QR code on the projector. Minimal logistics only: it's anonymous, nothing is kept on our server, and you can always speak aloud instead.
2. **Open submissions.** One short prompt, no categories, phrased as something to do: "Write down something you experienced, felt, or were surprised by in 2026." Nudges from a preset list appear as rotating placeholder text in the input; whenever submissions go quiet for about 10 seconds, the AI picks the nudge for the least-covered direction. If the mix is already varied, no nudge is shown. Participants first write as many private notes as they like, freely and without self-censoring; notes never leave the phone. Then they intentionally pick up to 3 to submit as cards (anything still in the writing box when they go to pick is saved as a note first, so nothing typed is lost), about 140 characters each (hard cap 200). A loose local check softly flags possible identifiers on the picked notes; the author decides. Once someone has shared all 3, the time warnings stop and their unshared notes stay listed, read-only, under what they shared, until they're wiped like any other private note. Projector shows "on your phone". Submissions get 5 minutes. Erik sees a live count; when the time is up he is asked "Ready for grouping?" and the window stays open until he confirms, and "+1 minute" extends it (and clears the prompt).
3. **Cluster.** The AI groups cards into themes, with near-duplicates in one group. Erik approves cluster names. The stream is ordered later, once reactions are in.
4. **React.** Each phone shows one card at a time, shuffled per person, so every distinct thought gets a fair chance. Near-duplicates in a group don't each need a full chance; their reactions are pooled. Tap one or more emojis, and optionally write a comment (up to 200 characters), then go to the next card. A comment alone is enough; a send with neither is refused. A comment is stored with no author, one per phone per card. "◀ Previous" goes back to change a card's reactions: the first send for a card returns a random receipt that the phone keeps, and sending again with it replaces those reactions and that card's comment (sending nothing with it clears both; a comment Erik removed stays removed). A receipt covers one card's reactions and is never tied to the phone, so a person's reactions stay unlinkable across cards. Changing doesn't use up the one pass. Each person makes one pass, one card per group. Reactions get 5 minutes, with "+1 minute" as for submissions. Erik is asked "Ready for the walk-through?" when the time is up, or as soon as every connected phone has finished; he confirms. Reactions are still accepted for 10 seconds after the room moves on, so a send in flight isn't lost.
5. **Walk through the stream (look-up phase).** Phones say "look up". It opens on an overview slide before the first card: titled "Themes" (so it's clear the AI grouped the cards and found what they share), biggest first, with "First reactions? Anything surprise you?", so the room can take in the whole before going card by card. Erik says aloud here that he sets the pace, the timers are hints he can override, and anyone can ask to stay or go back (except after the shred). The cards then advance by themselves on the projector, each showing its emoji mix and up to 5 of its comments (always in the same order, with "+N more" for the rest). Erik has Pause, Back and Next. The walk-through has a fixed finish, minute 21.5 on the talk clock (never shorter than 3 minutes): each card gets the walk-through's time left, less 20 seconds kept for the last slide, divided by the cards left (the overview counts as one), never under 15 seconds. A pause doesn't move the finish; it eats into later cards, and resuming gives the card a fresh share. If time runs out with cards left, it skips to the last slide, "Anything else? Out loud." (20 seconds), and then asks Erik "Ready for the finale?"; Back or Next after the walk-through's time is up holds the card until Erik resumes; skipped cards' reactions still count in the finale. A thin bar on each card drains as its time runs, with no numbers: it is the one per-card signal the room sees, so someone speaking can see it coming and Erik can pause. Erik sees the comments on the current and next card, so he can remove one before it is projected. Aim for 4–6 clusters. Erik's view shows "N cards left · ~M:SS each" throughout, so skipping is never a surprise. Phones keep "Stay" and "Go back", counted on the projector, so Erik can decide to pause. Erik's view also suggests a couple of open questions per card for when the room is quiet (never projected). People speak aloud by raising a physical hand.
6. **Last call.** Spoken only: "Anything else to say?" Out loud. There is no separate phase for it; the walk-through moves straight to the finale.
7. **Finale.** The room's whole emoji mix projected as one wordless visual, an emotional fingerprint of the year. A moment of quiet.
8. **Shred.** Everything is visibly destroyed and actually deleted. It opens with a plain "Delete everything?" dialog and a cursor clicking Delete, to say what is really happening on the server before the ritual shows it. Visual: a literal shredder; the pooled emojis, then the cards behind them, are pulled down through a slot and fall away as strips (narrow enough, about 1.2% of the screen width, that no whole word survives; they sway as they come out, flutter and crumple as they fall, and pile up in a mesh wastebasket), ending on a black screen and a short beat of silence (the last strips settle for about 2 seconds, then the fade, then 1.5 seconds of black), after which the closing screen fades in by itself. Back can't undo the shred, so it needs a confirm.
9. **Closing (optional).** What the app does, what was left out, and "what do you think?" The projector shows a QR code to a pinned GitHub Discussions thread for feedback (github.com/ert485/off-the-record/discussions/1). Posting there needs a GitHub account and is not anonymous, which is said in the thread itself; an anonymous feedback box in the app would contradict "nothing is kept", so there isn't one.

Talk clock: Erik starts it with one tap ("Start talk clock") at the first slide; it never restarts. Its length is 25 minutes, or whatever `TALK_MINUTES` is set to when the server starts. If he forgets, it starts by itself when the room first moves on from the demo, backdated to where the plan says that moment should be, so the pace marker is still meaningful.

The walk-through is the overrun risk: a lively one eats the closing and buffer first.

Pace indicator: an ahead/behind marker, measured on the talk clock, showing where the session is against where it should be to finish on time, with a short label ("on pace", "~2 min behind") and an ⓘ that explains it on tap. No phase names. Facilitator view only: on the projector, a visible "behind" would make people hold back.

Section timer: the projector shows the time left for the current part (submissions, reactions, walk-through), so the room knows how long a part is meant to take. It disappears when time runs out rather than showing "over", for the same reason as the pace indicator. Submissions and reactions count down to the time Erik was asked at (so "+1 minute" moves it); the walk-through's timer counts down to its fixed finish on the talk clock, so going back to it (from the finale, say) doesn't restart it. When a part is over and Erik hasn't confirmed, the timer is gone and the projector says "Waiting for the facilitator to confirm".

## App spec

Three views, one small server. Keep it minimal: about 40 users needs no heavy realtime infrastructure.

**What the app must get right**

These came out of building it and the practice runs. Check any change against them; the details further down are how each one is met today.

- **Every promise on screen is literally true.** Wording follows what the code actually does: "Notes you don't share never leave your phone", "Everything on our server is shredded tonight", notes "stay on this phone until you choose to share them". Where the app can't guarantee something (a phone that's offline at the shred), it tells the person what they can do ("To be sure, close this tab") rather than claiming more. A claim that can't be made true gets reworded, not kept.
- **Nothing a person does is lost or hidden from them.** Text typed but not saved still counts as a note. Shared cards stay visible as "Already shared". Reactions can be revisited and changed. No button leads to an empty step, and no action makes something silently disappear.
- **Every screen says what to do now and what happens next.** Prompts start with a verb. After an action, the phone says what's left ("You can still share 1 more") or that it's waiting for others. Controls that matter are visible where they're used, with accurate how-tos (the "＋ Add your own emoji" button and how to reach the emoji keyboard). Write for someone seeing it for the first time, with no explanation from the front.
- **Time is a visible hint, never pressure.** The room sees how long a part is meant to take, but never a per-card number, "over time" or "behind"; the walk-through's draining bar (no numbers) is the one per-card signal. "A visible hint" covers submissions and reactions; the walk-through is a timed reveal with a pause. Erik sees the pace and the cards left with each one's share, each explained on tap, and decides; timers are hints he overrides, and he says so aloud.
- **The facilitator view works at a glance for a first-time facilitator.** The same layout and colours in every phase; buttons say where they go ("Start reactions ▶", "◀ Back one card"); every number explains itself on tap (ⓘ) instead of headings filling the screen; status messages give the real reason (an empty round, not "no API key"); common actions take one tap ("Approve all"); nothing collapses or moves under a finger; nothing is shown that does nothing (no Back or Done after the shred).
- **Defaults keep Erik's influence, and pressure on the room, low.** Nudges start off. Last call invites speaking out loud. The walk-through opens on the themes as a whole before any single card. Prompts for a quiet room stay on Erik's screen.
- **The rituals feel real, and don't drag.** The shred has to read as actual destruction: a literal "Delete" moment, strips too narrow for any word to survive, physical motion into a bin. The finale's scale is continuous: the particles add up to the pools they become. Endings stay brief, and the closing fades in by itself.
- **Readable from the back of the room.** Anything the room has to read (the late-join QR caption, last call) is large and short. Longer explanations go in fine print or are said aloud.
- **Built to run again, by anyone.** After the shred, one confirmed step starts a new session. The code is public, and feedback goes to a public thread that says plainly it isn't anonymous.

**Design rules**

- No dot voting. Emoji reactions are the signal, and they capture mixed feelings.
- Emojis: 8 preset reactions (🙋 me too, 🫂 sending care, 😂 laughed, 🥲 bittersweet, 😢 sad, 😤 frustrated, 😰 anxious, 🎉 glad for you) plus any emoji from the phone keyboard (covers inside jokes).
- The reaction stack is shuffled per person so reactions spread evenly. Advancing is a plain "next", never a left/right swipe that feels like accept or reject.
- Too few cards (under about 10–15): stream every card and spend longer on each, still clustering near-duplicates. Too many: a curated stream with "+N similar".
- Phone vibrates on each phase change where the browser allows it (Android, after a first tap: the join screen has an "I'm here" button for that). iPhones don't support web vibration, so every phone also flashes its screen.
- Steering signals that don't cut a speaker off (stay, suggest back) show as live counts on the projector. There is no "move on" signal: projecting it while someone talks would be harsh. No "this one hit me": the emoji mix already shows how much a card landed.
- No digital raise hand; physical hands keep the room human.
- No open anonymous Q&A during the walk-through. Comments are written only while reacting, about the card being reacted to, and are shown (up to 5 per card) when the walk-through reaches that card.

**Build notes**

- One server, SQLite, SSE or 2-second polling. As built: Node 22 with no framework, the built-in `node:sqlite`, plain HTML and JavaScript with no build step. SSE sends each screen a full snapshot of what it may see (phones never receive hidden cards, comments, unapproved theme names or the pace marker), so a phone that drops off LTE just catches up. Two dependencies: the Anthropic SDK and a QR code library.
- Hosting: an EC2 instance in Erik's personal AWS account at `otr.tetl.ca` (DNS at the tetl.ca registrar points to an Elastic IP). Caddy in front for HTTPS with all of its logging discarded; the app logs nothing about requests. No EBS snapshots, no AWS Backup plan, no VPC flow logs, no load balancer. The instance is terminated after the talk, which destroys its disk.
- Phones keep private notes, and the cards they've already shared (listed separately as "Already shared" so people can still see what they sent), in the tab's sessionStorage: they survive a reload on a flaky connection. They're wiped when the shred reaches the phone or the tab closes. A phone that is offline at the shred can't hear about it, so every session has a random id, and a phone wipes notes written in a different session as soon as it reconnects. Closing the tab is the sure way, and the phone says so: on the unshared notes ("To be sure, close this tab") and on the shred and closing screens ("You can close this tab").
- Phones warn twice before submissions close: at 2 minutes ("leave time to pick what you share") and at 1 minute ("pick what to share now"), so a slow writer still has time to pick.
- Any emoji from the keyboard is offered as a clear "＋ Add your own emoji" button under the presets, which opens a box and says how to reach the emoji keyboard (a practice run found the bare box easy to miss). Emojis of your own that you've used on earlier cards appear as one-tap chips on later cards (up to 6, most recent first, kept on the phone and wiped like the notes); adding a 7th replaces the oldest one not picked on that card. The box takes emojis only: letters are cleared with a hint to switch to the emoji keyboard, and a preset typed from the keyboard selects that preset.
- The projector (Erik's laptop only) loads Noto Color Emoji from Google Fonts; participants' phones load nothing from third parties.
- Test on LTE; don't rely on venue wifi. Render emojis on the projector in one font (such as Noto Color Emoji) so phone styles don't clash.
- QR code must be readable from the back row; projector text legible at a distance.
- Rehearse twice. Tech run, solo, by Nov 18 (code near-final): laptop as facilitator plus 3–4 devices, one on LTE; check phase sync, back, clustering, real deletion, projector legibility. Full run, Nov 20–22, 5–10 people on their own phones on the real 25-minute clock; check timings, nudges, false flags, controls, finale and shred feel. Ask testers: "Did anything make you hold back?"

**Facilitator access (no logins)**

- "One facilitator" means one secret, not one device: a facilitator token, set as an env var for this one event.
- Facilitator view at a secret URL like `/facilitate?t=…`, bookmarked on both laptop and phone. Both work at once, so a crash never locks Erik out. Without the right token the URL returns "not found", and pages send no referrer, so the token can't leak through a link.
- Participants only ever get the join code. The facilitator view has no submit option; Erik can submit his own card from a participant tab like anyone else.

## Facilitation logic

Anonymizing happens locally on each phone, with no AI. The AI only clusters and orders cards after they're submitted.

**Pre-submission check (local, no AI)**

- Loose heuristics on the phone (case-insensitive matching against a list of local company names and a few thousand common first names, minus names that are also common words like "will" or "mark", and minus AI assistants' names like "Claude", which a practice run flagged) show a soft indicator: "You might have named or identified someone here. Consider rephrasing if so."
- The author decides whether to edit. Nothing is blocked, and the text stays on the phone until submitted.
- The same check applies to comments.
- No cloud AI anonymizing and no facilitator moderation queue. Accepted tradeoff: a card that slips past the heuristics may be projected. The only safety net is that Erik can hide any card before it's projected. Hidden cards leave the stream and reaction stack and are shredded with everything else.

**Clustering and the stream**

- Groups submitted cards into themes, including near-duplicates when there are few cards.
- Neutral cluster labels, approved by Erik before projection. Whenever submissions go quiet for about 10 seconds, the server re-clusters everything in the background and Erik's view shows the latest draft. On close, a draft with no newer cards is used as is; otherwise one final full run (about 5–10 seconds). Not built (cut for time): grouping runs once, when submissions close; with Haiku it took about 2 seconds for 12 cards in testing, and Erik's view shows "Grouping…" meanwhile.
- Orders cards into one stream: split clusters first, a few representative cards per cluster, the rest as "+N similar". Keep the ordering rule simple and transparent, since order is a form of editorial framing. The rule as built (`server/stream.js`): themes in order of total reactions; inside a theme, distinct thoughts in order of reactions; with 15 or fewer distinct thoughts, show them all, otherwise the top 3 per theme, the last one carrying "+N more like this". Near-duplicates always show as one card with "+N similar".
- Hidden cards are never sent to the AI. If clustering fails (or there's no API key), every card becomes its own group in one theme and Erik names it; a Retry button re-runs it. While the AI is grouping, Erik can "Skip the AI" and get that same fallback at once, so a slow or stuck API never holds up the room.
- Going back to grouping regroups only if cards came in that have no group yet. Hiding a card doesn't regroup, so reactions stay attached to their cards and approved names are kept.
- Budget about 10 seconds per card shown.

**Nudges (preset, any year)**

Shown as rotating placeholder text. As built (`server/nudges.js`): with fewer than 5 cards, phones rotate all six. After that, once submissions are quiet for 10 seconds, Claude Haiku tags each visible card with the directions it touches, and phones show only the nudge for the first direction no card covers; if every direction is covered, no nudge. Nudges start off in every session; Erik turns them on with the toggle, and his view says which nudge phones are showing. The AI matches cards to labels and shows the nudge for the least-covered one; "good" and "hard" are a balancing pair. No "someone who…" nudges, since they invite names. Refine after practice rounds.

| Label | Nudge text |
| --- | --- |
| outside work | Something away from the keyboard. |
| small moment | A small moment that stuck with you. |
| change | Something that changed for you. |
| connection | A time you felt connected, or didn't. |
| good | Something you're glad about. |
| hard | Something that was hard. |

**Finale (replaces the written summary)**

A written AI summary would turn the ending into production and have the AI speaking for the room. Instead, project the room's whole emoji mix as one wordless visual.

- An emotional fingerprint of the year: "this is all of us" without a deliverable. Visual: every reaction arrives as an emoji in the order reactions happened (about a 10-second replay), sized so that each type's particles together cover about the same area as the pool they become (so a handful of reactions fill the screen with big emojis, and many fill it with small ones), then pools into one emoji per type, sized by count (area, not radius). Rare keyboard emojis stay small. No count labels: size alone carries scale, keeping the finale wordless and number-free. Optional: a faint trail of the original particles around each pool (not built; cut for time).
- Let it sit for a moment, then shred it with everything else.
- No note-taker. Recording spoken points would undermine "off the record" and make people self-censor.

## Privacy and the shred

The shred is the core of the vibe: it makes anonymity believable and turns the retro into release rather than production.

Why it works: impermanence invites candor in a small community where everyone knows everyone; ending in destruction says the point was saying it and being heard; it's a ritual with a clear emotional close; and it leaves nothing to maintain.

**Requirements**

- No login, no names stored. Unsubmitted notes never leave the phone and are discarded: wiped when the shred reaches the phone or the tab closes (see Build notes for a phone that's offline at the shred).
- Cards are stored with no author and no timestamp, under random ids and in id order (never the order they arrived), so their order doesn't reveal which cards one phone submitted together. The server knows each phone only by a random id, and keeps counts against it (cards submitted, cards reacted to), never which cards or reactions.
- The shred deletes the SQLite database and everything else in the data directory (the app writes nothing anywhere else), then keeps the pooled emojis and card text in memory only for the projector's 30-second animation. `test/shred.test.js` proves it: it runs a whole session and then checks that no file, log line or screen still holds any of it, and that the server printed no IP address.
- Identifier checks run locally; the author decides exactly what gets shared.
- After submission, cards go to Anthropic's API (Claude Haiku 5.5, chosen for speed) for clustering. Softened promise, stated in one sentence: the provider doesn't train on the cards and deletes them within its retention window (about 30 days); everything on our server is gone tonight. Checked Nov 19 against Anthropic's privacy center: API inputs and outputs are deleted within 30 days, except content its safety systems flag for a usage-policy violation, which can be kept up to 2 years. Haiku 5.5 is not one of the "Covered Models" with special retention rules.
- The shred must be literally true on our side: delete all server data, including backups and any files on disk. Say so.
- Turn off server access logs (no IP addresses kept) and skip hosting analytics.
- Be honest about the limit: in a room of about 40 people, a card's content can identify someone even with names removed.
- No published results.

**Trust disclosure (keep it short)**

One slide or sentence in plain language, plus the repo link and QR code for anyone who wants to check. On the projector, a short version is big ("Anonymous: no logins, no names. Notes you don't share never leave your phone. Everything on our server is shredded tonight.", each line literally true) and the full disclosure below it is fine print, read aloud. No lengthy security defence; it would read as suspicious and eat the minimal opening. Optionally, one respected community member reviews the repo beforehand and Erik mentions it in one line. The disclosure names every power Erik has, including that he sees cards and comments before they're shown and can remove any of them.

Draft (one slide, read aloud during Join, about 20 seconds):

> Anonymous: no logins, no names, no IP logs. Your notes stay on your phone unless you pick them to submit. Submitted cards go to Anthropic (Claude Haiku) for grouping; they don't train on them and delete them within 30 days. I see cards and comments before they're shown and can remove any of them to prevent abuse. Everything on our server is shredded tonight. Code: \[short repo URL\]

Controls are explained just in time, not here: at the start of the walk-through, one line that phones can ask Erik to stay or go back.

**Facilitator cues (said aloud, not on screen)**

From the practice run, things Erik says rather than the app showing:

- At the start: feedback on how to improve this is for the end (there's a QR code then, and Erik stays after the next talk), but if you're confused about how it works, stop me and ask.
- Don't read the slides aloud; let people read and ask.
- On the themes slide, before the first card: I set the pace, the timers are hints I can override, and you can ask me to go back at any point (except after the shred). The room's reactions set the pace, along with the timers only I can see.
- Short, honest reactions are enough. Knowing you're not alone is the point; not every card needs a discussion.
